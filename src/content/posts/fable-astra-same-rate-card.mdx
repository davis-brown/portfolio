Claude Fable 5.1 and GPT-6 Astra bill at exactly the same rate: $10 per million
input tokens, $50 per million output. If you are comparing sticker prices, there
is nothing to compare.

So it was strange to read Artificial Analysis put Astra at parity with Fable on
their Intelligence Index at roughly 40% of the cost, and at about 60% on the
Coding Agent Index. Same rate, materially smaller bill.

The gap is token efficiency. Reasoning models spend a variable number of tokens
thinking before they answer, and that count, not the rate, is the line item that
moves. It means you cannot forecast spend from a pricing page. You have to
measure tokens per completed task on your own workload, which is a different and
much less convenient exercise than reading two numbers off a table.

## On code, it is closer to a tie than the launch posts suggest

Deep SWE and Frontier Code put the two at roughly the same level. Terminal Bench
4.0 is where Astra separates, at about 57.9% against Fable's 55.8%, with GPT-5.6
Sol back at 37.3%.

That last comparison is the useful one. The generational jump is large; the gap
between the two current frontier models is about two points on one benchmark.
Two points is not an architecture decision. If you are choosing between these
because of coding benchmarks, you are choosing on noise.

## Where they actually diverge

The interesting differences are not on the coding leaderboards.

Every's review landed on a split verdict: Fable for writing, for long documents,
and for analysis you have to defend. Astra for math, for agents that work inside
applications and fill forms, and for short tasks at volume.

The sharpest single result I saw was Andon Labs running both on Vending-Bench, a
long-horizon business simulation. Astra finished with an average bank balance of
$15,515 against Fable's $5,422. Nearly three times, on a task that rewards
staying coherent over a long run rather than being right once.

If you are building something agentic that has to hold a thread across many
steps, that margin is hard to argue with.

## The failure mode matters more than the score

The detail I keep coming back to is not a number. Reviewers reported that
Fable's output was easier to build on without first undoing things it had added
unasked, while Astra was easier to steer through back and forth, though it
needed less context up front to get moving.

That is the difference between a model you brief carefully and accept, and one
you correct as you go. Which you want is not a quality judgment. It depends
entirely on whether a human is in the loop.

For an agent running unattended at three in the morning, a model that does
roughly the right thing and stops is worth more than one that does slightly
better work but requires a conversation to get there. For a developer pairing
with it interactively, the opposite.

## How I would actually choose

Treat it like any other dependency decision, because that is what it is.

**Is a human in the loop?** If yes, steerability wins and the back and forth is
cheap. If no, you want the model that needs the least correction, because there
is nobody there to correct it.

**Is the task long-horizon and agentic?** The Vending-Bench spread is the
clearest signal either way that I have seen.

**Does the output get defended to someone?** A summary that a person will take
into a meeting is a different product than a form that gets filled.

**Is it high volume and short?** Then measure tokens per task, not rate. That is
where the cost difference lives, and it is workload-specific enough that
published indices are a prior rather than a result.

## The part nobody benchmarks

None of this covers the things that decide whether a feature works on Monday
morning: rate limits under real concurrency, latency variance when the provider
is busy, and how quickly you find out when something is degraded.

I have spent enough time on production incidents to be more interested in the
shape of the failure than the height of the score. A model that is two points
better and occasionally unavailable is worse than one that is two points worse
and boring. Benchmarks measure the good case. Your users experience the bad one.

Both of these are good enough that the benchmark is no longer the interesting
question. Pick on failure mode, measure cost on your own traffic, and keep the
integration loose enough that switching is a config change rather than a
project.
